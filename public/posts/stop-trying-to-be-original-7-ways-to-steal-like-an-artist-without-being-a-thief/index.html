Stop Trying to Be Original: 7 Ways to Steal Like an Artist (Without Being a Thief)

Seven ideas from Austin Kleon's Steal Like an Artist on copying your heroes, making what you wish existed, and getting more done by being boring.

Every artist gets asked where their ideas come from, and Austin Kleon says the honest answer is three words: “I steal them.”

That sounds like a confession, but it works better as a method. In Steal Like an Artist, Kleon argues that nothing comes from nowhere, and that the people who look most original are usually the ones who borrowed from the most places. It’s a short book for anyone who makes things, whether that’s songs, spreadsheets or sales pitches.

Here are the seven ideas I’d pass on.

1. Nothing is original, so become a curator#

Every new idea is a remix of older ones, which makes your taste your main tool.

Kleon borrows an art-school trick. Draw two parallel lines and ask how many lines you see. There are two, plus the empty space between them: 1 + 1 = 3. Ideas combine the same way, and so do you. You’re a mashup of the teachers, music and books you chose to let in. Writer Jonathan Lethem, he notes, says that when people call something original, nine times out of ten they simply don’t know the sources.

The difference between a collector and a hoarder is selectivity. Hoarders keep everything, artists keep what they love. And don’t try to swallow your whole field at once. Study one thinker you love, learn everything about them, then study three people they loved, and keep climbing. Once you’ve built your tree, grow your own branch.

Share this: start a swipe file, a folder or scrapbook of things worth stealing, and write down the first hero for your tree.

2. Copy the thinking, not the surface#

Copying is how everyone learns, so aim at how your heroes see, not how their work looks.

Writers copy the alphabet, musicians play scales, painters reproduce masterpieces. The Beatles started as a cover band, and Paul McCartney says they began writing their own songs partly to stop other bands from playing their set. Kleon’s rule is to copy many heroes, not one. Copy one author and it’s plagiarism, goes the line he quotes, but copy many and it’s research.

The best part comes when you fail. Kobe Bryant stole his moves from tapes of players he admired, but his body couldn’t pull them off the same way, so he adapted them. Kleon retells Conan O’Brien’s version of the pattern: Johnny Carson tried to be Jack Benny and became Carson, David Letterman tried to be Carson and became Letterman, and O’Brien tried to be Letterman and became O’Brien. Our inability to make perfect copies is where our own voice hides.

Share this: copy a hero’s work closely, note exactly where your version falls short, then amplify those differences.

3. Write the book you want to read#

Instead of making what you know, make what you wish already existed.

Kleon’s example is humbling. Jurassic Park came out on his tenth birthday, and the next day he typed up his own sequel on the family computer. He didn’t know it, but he was writing fan fiction. The real sequel arrived years later and disappointed him, because it never matches the one in your head.

That’s his point. Advice to write what you know tends to produce stories where nothing happens. Write what you like instead. Musician Bradford Cox, as a kid, recorded fake versions of his favorite band’s upcoming albums, then compared them with the real ones. Many of those songs eventually became songs for his own band, Deerhunter.

Share this: think of your favorite creators and ask what they missed, then go make that.

4. Start before you know who you are#

You don’t find yourself first and then make things. You find yourself by making things.

Kleon says that if he’d waited to understand who he was, he’d still be sitting around figuring it out. He’s blunt about impostor syndrome, the feeling of being a phony who’s winging it: nobody doing real creative work knows where the good stuff comes from. They show up and do the work every day. His take on the old fake-it-until-you-make-it line has two readings, and he likes both. Act like the person you want to be, and keep pretending to make something until you actually do.

He also finds freedom in being unknown. When nobody’s watching, there’s no image to manage and no paycheck on the line, so you can experiment and get better. Once attention and money arrive, you never get that back. His advice is to enjoy your obscurity while it lasts, and use it.

Share this: make the first ugly version this week, and don’t announce it.

5. Put your hands back in your work#

The computer is good for editing and publishing, but bad at generating ideas.

Kleon says it’s too easy to hit delete, so the computer turns us into perfectionists who edit ideas before we’ve had them. Cartoonist Tom Gauld, he reports, stays off the computer until most of his thinking is done, because once it’s involved, everything is on a path to being finished.

Kleon’s own setup is two desks. The analog desk holds markers, pens, pencils, paper, index cards and newspaper, and nothing electronic is allowed. The digital desk has the laptop, scanner and drawing tablet. His first book, Newspaper Blackout, was made hands first, then computer, then hands, then computer. Every poem was made from a newspaper article and a permanent marker.

Share this: spend about $10 on paper, pens and sticky notes, and set up an analog corner. Stand up, pin things on the wall, and move to the screen only once you have something to execute.

6. Be boring so your work can be wild#

Creative energy is finite, so protect it with a dull, steady life.

Kleon calls himself a boring guy with a nine-to-five job. The image of the genius burning out young, he says, is for superhumans and people who want to die young. His version: eat breakfast, sleep, go for long walks. Even Patti Smith, he says, tells young artists to go to the dentist.

He’s a fan of the day job, too. It pays the bills, puts you around other people to learn from, and gives you a routine. Money worries cost you freedom in your art, so he recommends a budget, packing your lunch, and holding on to what you earn. For the work itself, he leans on Jerry Seinfeld’s method: hang a wall calendar showing the whole year and put a big X on each day you do the work. Soon you have a chain, and your only job is not to break it.

Share this: buy a calendar tonight, pick one daily task, and start the chain.

7. Creativity is subtraction#

Limits don’t kill creativity, they cure creative block.

Kleon says the idea that you can do anything is terrifying, and that endless possibility paralyzes. The cure is to add constraints: write a song on your lunch break, paint with one color, start a business with no start-up capital. His favorite example is Dr. Seuss, who wrote The Cat in the Hat using only 236 different words. His editor then bet he couldn’t write a book with just 50, and Seuss won the bet with Green Eggs and Ham.

This also closes the loop with the first idea. If you’re a mashup of what you let in, you’re equally defined by what you leave out. Curating influences is subtraction, and so is choosing what not to copy and what not to put in your work.

Share this: add one constraint to your next project, such as less time, fewer materials or fewer words.

Conclusion#

You’re not original because you invent from nothing. You’re original because of what you choose to take in, what you choose to copy, and what you choose to leave out.

  • Start a swipe file this week.
  • Pick one hero and copy their thinking, not just their style.
  • Make the thing you wish existed.
  • Do your first draft on paper.
  • Mark a calendar every day you work.
  • Add one constraint to your next project.

One caveat#

This is a short, opinionated book built on the author’s own experience and a lot of borrowed quotes, not on research. It dates from 2012, and its advice on sharing, which boils down to putting your work on the Internet, is far less of a guarantee of being seen today. Kleon also separates copying to learn from passing off someone’s work as yours, but he doesn’t give you a test for where homage ends and theft begins. You have to draw that line yourself. He admits as much: take what you can use and leave the rest.

Source: Austin Kleon, Steal Like an Artist: 10 Things Nobody Told You About Being Creative (2012).
