Pericles ran Athens for over three decades by assuming everyone around him, including himself, was fundamentally irrational. He was right, and it's the whole reason the city had a golden age under him and fell apart within a decade of his death.

That's the opening case in Robert Greene's *The Laws of Human Nature*, and it sets the tone for the whole book: humans like to believe we're rational agents making reasoned choices, when in fact most of our decisions are emotional reactions dressed up afterward as logic. Greene spends 500-plus pages walking through the specific, recurring ways this plays out — in ourselves, in the people we trust, and in the groups we belong to — using historical figures as case studies instead of abstract theory.

Here are six of the sharpest ideas in it.

## You think you're rational. You're not.

Pericles noticed something strange about Athenian politics: every leader around him believed he was acting rationally, had sound reasons for his decisions, and could always explain afterward why things went wrong. And yet the city lurched from crisis to crisis. The problem wasn't a lack of intelligence — it was that intelligence was being deployed in service of emotion, not instead of it.

Pericles built his entire career around training himself out of this. He refused to make decisions while under the influence of a strong emotion, physically removing himself from heated debates until he calmed down. For decades it worked: Athens expanded its wealth, built the Parthenon, and avoided the needless wars other leaders kept pushing for. Then Pericles died of plague in the second year of the war with Sparta, and without him the city voted, within a few decades, to slaughter the men of a surrendered island and launch a catastrophic expedition against Syracuse that ended the Athenian empire for good. Same citizens, same institutions — just without the one man who'd trained himself to interrupt his own emotional reactions.

**Share this:** The test isn't whether you feel rational — everyone does, including the people making terrible decisions. The test is whether you have a practice for catching yourself before you act on a strong emotion. Greene's own suggestion: treat any decision made in a state of excitement, anger, or anxiety as provisional, and revisit it once the feeling has passed.

## Judge people by their patterns, not their pitch

Howard Hughes inherited a profitable tool company, taught himself to fly, and built a public image as the ultimate self-made American maverick. He was also, demonstrably, a catastrophic manager — and the pattern was visible decades before his death. On *Hell's Angels*, he fired three directors, ran up a budget of $3.8 million against revenue of under $2 million, and ended up directing it himself. On a WWII military contract to build reconnaissance planes, he burned through $6 million of a $9 million budget without delivering a single plane, and the Air Force canceled the order. In the 1960s, in almost the exact same sequence, he underbid the Army for a helicopter contract, failed to deliver on schedule, and lost $90 million trying to win the follow-on order.

Three different industries, three decades apart, same exact failure mode: hire someone competent, undermine their authority within weeks, insist on approving every detail personally, watch the project collapse. People who worked with him kept getting fooled by the same things — his charm, his technical fluency, his reputation — because they judged him by his résumé and his pitch instead of by what he had actually done the last three times.

**Share this:** Before trusting someone with something important — a job, a partnership, your money — look at what they did the last two or three times they were in a similar position, not what they say they'll do this time. Greene's line for it: character is destiny, and nobody does something just once.

## Everyone has a Shadow, including the nice ones

Richard Nixon spent his public life cultivating an image of himself — "RN," he called it in his notebooks — as a statesman in the mold of Lincoln, driven by his Quaker mother's ideals. Privately, late at night with his staff, he returned obsessively to old grievances: the 1960 election he narrowly lost, the East Coast elites who'd snubbed him for attending Whittier instead of Harvard, the journalists he was convinced were out to destroy him. That repressed resentment didn't disappear because he'd built a nicer public persona over it — it came out sideways, in a secret White House taping system and, eventually, in Watergate.

Greene calls this repressed, denied part of the personality the Shadow, borrowing the term from Carl Jung. The more polished and consistently "nice" someone's public front is, the thicker their Shadow tends to be — not because niceness is fake, but because something has to be done with the aggression, insecurity, and resentment every person actually has.

**Share this:** The people easiest to misjudge aren't the openly difficult ones — they're the ones whose public persona never cracks at all. Watch for the moments when someone's voice or face changes for a split second under pressure; that's the Shadow leaking through, and it tells you more than months of pleasant interactions do.

## That envy you're denying is running your social media feed

Studies cited in the book have found that even monkeys experience envy — it's that deeply wired into social animals. Yet almost nobody will admit to feeling it; we experience the initial pang and almost instantly convert it into something more acceptable, like irritation or moral disapproval of the person who has what we want. Greene points to Robert Rubin, Goldman Sachs co-head and later U.S. Treasury Secretary, as someone who understood this instinctively: in meetings where he was clearly the most knowledgeable person in the room, he made a habit of asking junior staff for their opinions first and listening as if he meant it. Colleagues described him as nearly impossible to resent, in an industry built on resentment.

The book also makes a point that reads differently now than it might have before social media was the dominant way people compare their lives: a feed is a highly curated highlight reel, and constant exposure to other people's best moments manufactures low-grade envy on a scale humans never had to process before.

**Share this:** When you notice the specific flash of irritation at someone else's good news, don't suppress it — name it as envy and ask what it's telling you about what you actually want. Then do the less natural thing: try to feel genuine pleasure at their success instead of resentment. Nietzsche had a word for this, *Mitfreude* — joying with — and the book argues it's rare enough that practicing it actively builds trust other people can feel.

## Groups turn you into someone you wouldn't recognize alone

In 1966, a studious teenager named Gao Jianhua was attending one of China's most prestigious schools, dreaming of becoming a writer. Within months, as the Cultural Revolution swept through, he was patrolling the campus as a Red Guard while teachers he liked and respected were forced into "struggle meetings," paraded in dunce caps, and in at least one case, driven to suicide. Jianhua didn't start out as someone capable of any of this. Neither did most of his classmates. What changed was that they were operating inside a group dynamic that rewarded escalating zealotry and punished hesitation — and normal, previously decent people adjusted their behavior accordingly, in real time, without fully registering what was happening to them.

Greene's point isn't that this was a uniquely Chinese or uniquely political phenomenon. It's that groups reliably pull individuals toward more primitive, more conformist, more emotionally reactive versions of themselves — in offices and online communities as much as in revolutions, just with lower stakes.

**Share this:** When you notice yourself adopting a group's intensity about something faster than you'd normally form an opinion on your own, that's the signal to slow down, not speed up. Ask whether you'd hold the same position if you encountered the facts alone, with nobody watching.

## Thinking about death more often is the fix, not the problem

Flannery O'Connor was twenty-five when a doctor finally told her the truth her mother had been concealing: she had the same lupus that had killed her father at forty-five. Rather than retreating, she described the diagnosis as a second "bullet in the side" and moved home to her family's farm to write with total focus, producing *Wise Blood* and the short stories that made her one of the sharpest American writers of her era, almost entirely under the shadow of a disease she knew would likely kill her young. It did, in 1964, when she was thirty-nine.

Greene uses her, along with Fyodor Dostoyevsky's account of standing in front of a firing squad before a last-second reprieve, to argue for something that sounds morbid and isn't: deliberately and regularly confronting your own mortality, rather than pushing the thought away, is what cuts through the petty distractions and manufactured urgency most people spend their lives on. He calls the resulting feeling — the sense of being briefly, improbably alive inside something far larger than yourself — the Sublime, and argues it's accessible through smaller doses too: a night sky, a vast landscape, genuine contemplation of how unlikely your own existence was.

**Share this:** Once a week, actually sit with the fact that you will die, specifically and without euphemism, instead of deflecting the thought the way you probably do by instinct. People who do this report it doesn't produce despair — it produces urgency about what actually matters and indifference toward what doesn't.

## Conclusion

> Almost none of the conflict in your life is caused by other people's stated reasons for what they do. It's caused by the emotions, patterns, and repressed Shadow underneath those reasons — in them, and in you.

A short checklist to carry out of this one:

- Before a big decision, ask whether you're currently calm enough to trust your own reasoning — if not, wait.
- When evaluating someone important, pull up their actual track record instead of their pitch.
- Notice the split-second crack in someone's composure; it tells you more than their words do.
- Name your envy out loud to yourself the moment you feel it, rather than relabeling it as something more flattering.
- Before adopting a group's intensity about something, check whether you'd hold that position alone.
- Once a week, sit with your own mortality on purpose instead of pushing the thought away.

One honest caveat: Greene writes almost entirely through historical anecdote rather than citation-heavy research, and he's working from psychoanalytic frameworks — Jung's Shadow, repression — that have fallen out of favor in mainstream academic psychology even where the underlying observations still ring true. Treat the stories as illustrations of real patterns, not as proof in the scientific sense.

*Source: The Laws of Human Nature by Robert Greene, 2018.*
